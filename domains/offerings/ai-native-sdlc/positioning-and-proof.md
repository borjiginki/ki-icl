# Positioning and proof

## Who buys this cut

The head of software engineering, platform engineering, or digital services in an organization large enough that consistency cannot come from individual expertise. The buying trigger is usually one of:

- **AI pressure from above** with no credible engineering answer beyond a copilot rollout
- **A documentation or knowledge problem** the org has already diagnosed itself — often arriving as *"we need to build a knowledge base before we can use AI properly"*, which is the right instinct and our best possible opening
- **Delivery throughput** that has stopped responding to more headcount

Qualification is the same honest filter as the main offering: a named owner, a named high-volume process, and leadership air-cover. Absent those, we say so and stop.

## Reading the room

Software-engineering audiences are pragmatic and usually skeptical of vision theater. Go concrete fast — this is not the venue for a broad AI-landscape tour.

Two adjustments that reliably help:

- **Anchor on peers, not on the phrase "AI-native company."** That phrase sounds far away to an engineering audience. Acknowledge it, then immediately ground it in what is already running at comparable organizations.
- **Assume they are three steps ahead.** When a client has already concluded that documentation is the foundation, the job is to validate that instinct, give it structure, show it running, and sell the path — not to explain the premise back to them.

## What we compete against

**The copilot rollout.** Licenses and a few percent, single-player. We sell the jump to a redesigned process with a closed loop — and the ask that proves the difference is a working loop, not a license count.

**The platform vendors.** They ship the substrate, the access, the SSO and the compliance, and they ship it well. They do not ship the write-back context layer or the learning loop today — our software edge, and honestly commoditizable. They will never ship the process refounding, because it does not exist until it has been done with the client's own engineers on their own delivery process.

**Classic consulting.** An assessment deck that peaks on handover day. Ours starts as a working loop and improves from there.

## What we can show

**Two loops in production** at a multi-thousand-person energy company, both drawing on one governed foundation, running on that organization's existing enterprise stack. This is the strongest asset this cut has: it is not a slideware architecture, it is a build we can walk through.

**We run this way ourselves.** KI group, around fifty people, works from its own shared context layer — offers, decks and analyses agent-drafted from shared context, with senior review confirming the quality beats individual work. Expect the immediate follow-up: *"how does that scale to five thousand people?"* — have the scale bridge ready.

**Measured results — internal only.** The reference programme's own measurements are strong, and they are the reason this cut converts. They are **not cleared for client-facing print**: use them verbally, and check clearance before putting any of them on a slide or in a document that leaves the building. Ask before you use a figure externally.

## Naming and disclosure

Written material stays anonymized to vertical plus scale, as above. Client names are shared verbally and only once naming has been cleared for that specific reference — one deck having named a client once does not generalize to a standing clearance. For cleared, named references, see the `case-studies` domain.

Product walkthroughs built for pitches are **illustrative mock-ups** unless a slide explicitly says otherwise, and they must be labeled as such in the room. Never present a mock as a screenshot of a running system.
